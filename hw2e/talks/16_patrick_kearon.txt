Patrick Kearon (Of the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles)
Jesus Lives to Lift
A few weeks ago, President Dieter F. Uchtdorf took me aside and said, “Patrick, your talks—they’re OK, but where is the aviation?” So here’s just a little aviation.
I spent much of my childhood at boarding school in England looking up at aeroplanes crossing the sky, dreaming of flying home to my parents thousands of miles away in Arabia. I yearned to fly! I wanted to become a fighter pilot.
The Royal Air Force was recruiting pilots, and the fastest way for me to fly was learning to glide.
A glider is a lightweight aircraft with long, slender wings—and no engine—that stays aloft by riding within rising currents of air.
So in my mid-teens I spent part of two summers at an airfield in Oxfordshire learning to fly a glider with the air force. First I learned to fly “straight and level,” then to make stable turns, next to take off towed by a tug plane, and finally to land safely back at the airfield. Only then was I ready to learn to stay in the air, climb, and eventually soar—all without an engine!
The crucial skill in gliding is finding rising air. You cannot see it, but you can see what it does. I was taught to look for cumulus clouds because they often mark where warm air is rising, and I learned to fly toward them.
But even more important than using visual cues to find rising air is to learn to feel it. While flying along, I would begin to feel that I was being lifted by an unseen force. This rising air is called “lift.” One wing would tip upward, and I would turn toward it, into the lift.
I was taught how to stay in lift, circling upward to gain altitude, and to often join other pilots who had found lift, climbing with them in the same invisible column of rising air.
To be flying is fantastic, but climbing in lift and soaring with no engine is almost beyond description. For me it was beautiful—with only the whistle of the wind, the occasional creak from the glider, and the magnificent sensation of being drawn upward!
Just as there is rising air, there is also sinking air, called “sink.” It likewise cannot be seen, but it can be felt, and I quickly learned to avoid it. Gliding into sink can feel like falling, sometimes quickly. It shortens the distance you might fly and may threaten a safe landing. When I encountered sink, I learned to turn away decisively.
A decade after those gliding days, and not far into my journey of faith, I recognised that the principles of gliding I had learned in my teens have significant similarities with spiritual principles: We must avoid sink and find lift.
At the heart of our faith is the glorious truth that we have an adoring Father in Heaven, whose Son, our Saviour, loves to lift!
